content_legacy: '## English


  In the "Guiyi" chapter, Mozi draws a pointed comparison between merchants and scholars:
  "Merchants traveling to the four directions—if goods are twice the price, they will
  go despite the difficulties at passes and bridges and the dangers of bandits and
  thieves. Now a scholar sitting and speaking of righteousness has no difficulties
  at passes and bridges, no dangers from bandits and thieves, yet the profit is immeasurable—and
  still they do not act. Is this not the case that the scholar''s calculation of advantage
  is inferior to the merchant''s examination of profit?"


  The argument is rhetorically devastating: merchants face real physical dangers yet
  pursue profit zealously, while scholars face no obstacles yet neglect the far greater
  benefit of righteousness. This exposes a fundamental irrationality in the scholar''s
  behavior—their apparent caution is actually foolishness, their supposed difficulty
  is nothing compared to the merchant''s trials.


  This passage reflects Mozi''s broader utilitarian framework: if righteousness truly
  benefits the world, then abandoning it for ease represents a profound failure of
  rational self-interest. The scholar who will not act for righteousness while the
  merchant acts for mere profit demonstrates that scholars do not truly believe in
  what they profess. Their inaction is evidence that their claimed commitment to righteousness
  is hollow.

《墨子·貴義》篇載墨子以商賈與士人相較：「商人之四方，市賈倍徙，雖有關梁之難、盜賊之危，必爲之。今士坐而言義，無關梁之難、盜賊之危，此爲倍徙不可勝計，然而不爲。則士之計利，不若商人之察也。」

此論甚烈：商賈冒關梁之難、盜賊之危以趨倍徙之利，而士人坐而言義，無險可畏，猶不爲之。是士人計較利害，反不如商賈之明察也。

墨子此論，蓋以其功利主義之立場：義之利於天下，莫大於此。若天下莫爲義，則士人猶不爲之，是其口言義而心不然也。商賈之於利，猶有奔走之勤；士人之於義，乃安逸而不行。是豈眞知義之爲貴哉？

此亦見墨子「貴義」之本意：義之貴，在於其實利，而非空言。若口言義而不行，則是賤之也，非貴之也。
