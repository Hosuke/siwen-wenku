content_legacy: '## English


  The Chapter on Black Robes contains an important philosophical observation about
  the inseparability of speech and action: "苟有車，必見其軾；苟有衣，必見其敝；人苟或言之，必聞其聲；苟或行之，必見其成。"


  This passage employs a series of analogies to illustrate that words and deeds are
  intrinsically connected, like a cart and its crossbar, or a garment and its eventual
  wear. Just as one cannot have a cart without seeing its crossbar, and one cannot
  wear clothing without it eventually wearing out, so too are verbal statements and
  physical actions inseparable. If one speaks, one''s voice will be heard; if one
  acts, results will be visible.


  The context in the Book of Rites connects this to the ideal of the junzi (君子), who
  maintains consistency between what he says and does. The passage concludes with
  a reference to theode Songs (《葛覃》): "服之無射" (wearing it without ever tiring of it),
  suggesting that when one''s conduct aligns with one''s words, it becomes natural
  and unforced.


  This principle warns against the hypocrisy of saying one thing and doing another,
  a theme that runs throughout the chapter''s discussion of proper governance and
  personal cultivation.

《禮記·緇衣》載：「苟有車，必見其軾；苟有衣，必見其敝；人苟或言之，必聞其聲；苟或行之，必見其成。」又引《葛覃》云：「服之無射。」

此節以車輿衣裳為喻，闡明言行相須之理。車有則有軾，衣穿則有敝，言發則有聲，行施則有成：四者相因，不可分離。聖賢論學，必貴言行相符；修己治人，首重表裏如一。

苟有車而無軾，車不成其為車；苟有言而無行，言不成其為言。是以君子言之於口，行之於身，終始一貫，表裏相副。《詩》云「服之無射」，言苟能言行相應，則其德之盛，有非朝夕可盡者矣。
