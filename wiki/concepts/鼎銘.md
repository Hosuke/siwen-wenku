## English

The text includes a substantial discussion of the practice of *ding ming* (鼎銘), inscriptions cast onto ritual bronze vessels. This practice represents a sophisticated Confucian theory of how moral example and familial memory are preserved across generations.

The fundamental principle is articulated as follows: "The inscription is a self-naming. Through self-naming, one proclaims and extols the beauty of one's ancestors and makes it bright for future generations." This passage establishes that the purpose of vessel inscriptions is not mere record-keeping but moral pedagogy—commending virtue so that descendants may emulate it.

The text identifies three types of ancestors that require different treatments in inscriptions:
- **Virtuous ancestors**: Their good deeds must be proclaimed (稱美)
- **Faulty ancestors**: Their faults must not be mentioned (不稱惡)

This asymmetry reflects the psychology of the filial heart: "This is the heart of the filial son, the filial grandson. Only the worthy can do this."

The inscription also serves the self-cultivation of the inscribers themselves. By composing the text, they draw near to their ancestors (身比焉), thereby practicing reverence; by presenting it to later generations, they fulfill their duty of education (明示後世，教也). Thus the act of inscription is itself a moral practice, binding the commemorator to the commemorated and the past to the future.

A complete example from the text—attributed to the Wei state official Kong Cui (孔悝)—illustrates the formulaic structure: a ruler addresses his minister, recounting the achievements of the minister's ancestors, then charging him to continue their legacy and inscribe it upon the ritual vessels.
