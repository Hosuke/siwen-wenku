  ## English


  The chapter on Laws of Sacrifice in the Book of Rites establishes the fundamental
  principles and regulations governing sacrificial ceremonies in ancient Chinese ritual
  practice. This text articulates the hierarchical system of ancestral worship, the
  classification of spirits requiring veneration, and critically, the ethical criteria
  determining which individuals merit inclusion in the sacrificial canon.


  The core philosophical principle, expressed as 「法施於民，則祀之」 ("Those who govern in
  accordance with the people shall be sacrificed to"), establishes merit-based deification.
  This principle mandates sacrifice for those who: (1) established beneficial laws
  for the people; (2) served the state unto death; (3) secured the nation through
  great labor; (4) averted catastrophic disasters; (5) protected against major calamities.
  The text illustrates this principle through the deification of cultural heroes including
  the agrarian deity 厲山氏, the earth deity 后土, the grain deity 稷, and multiple sage
  kings and ministers from 黄帝 through 武王. The passage also outlines the Five Spirits
  system for celestial worship (sun, moon, stars, rain, drought) and the Seven Offerings
  hierarchy (司命, 中霤, 國門, 國行, 泰厲, 戶, 灶), each tiered by social rank from the Son of
  Heaven down to commoners. The foundational laws governing ancestral temples—from
  the seven-shrine system for kings to the single-shrine system for officials—further
  demonstrate how sacrifice operates as a comprehensive framework integrating political
  legitimacy, spiritual authority, and social order.


  The text presents sacrifice not merely as ritual performance but as the primary
  mechanism for preserving collective memory, rewarding virtue, and transmitting cultural
  values across generations. The five categories of spirits—celestial bodies, meteorological
  phenomena, and natural features like mountains, forests, rivers, and valleys—each
  receive appropriate veneration according to their nature and significance to human
  civilization. 少牢祭四時，寒暑之氣有坎壇以祭。山林、川谷、丘陵能出雲、為風雨、見怪物，皆曰神，故有天下者祭百神。


  這些祭祀對象形成完整的體系。天神系統包括日月星，地祇系統涵蓋山川丘陵。對於有天下者而言，所有神靈皆在祭祀範圍。諸侯則按其領地範圍祭祀——在則祭之，不在則不祭。

  This sacred system divides spirits into five categories based on their nature and
  function. Celestial spirits receive worship at designated locations—sun and moon
  at the royal palace, stars at their nocturnal shrines, rain and drought at the rain
  ceremonies. Meteorological phenomena are addressed through seasonal altars at specific
  sites. The final category encompasses terrestrial spirits inhabiting mountains,
  forests, rivers, and valleys, who manifest as clouds, wind, rain, and omens, commanding
  the veneration of all under heaven.


  The system distinguishes between universal rulers, who must honor all spirits, and
  feudal lords, who sacrifice only to those spirits within their own domains. This
  hierarchical structure of spirit worship—from the celestial bodies down through
  the meteorological and terrestrial realms—reveals how ancient Chinese cosmology
  organized the spiritual world according to the perceived power and scope of each
  deity''s influence.
