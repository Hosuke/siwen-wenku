  "Women weep and beat their breasts; men bow their foreheads to the ground" — this
  passage from *Liji · Wen Sang* delineates the different mourning expressions prescribed
  for men and women.


  The text states: women weep with sorrow and beat their breasts, injuring their hearts;
  men weep with sorrow and touch their foreheads to the ground, showing no regard
  for their appearance. This is the ultimate expression of grief.


  The text further explains that those with physical disabilities cannot fully perform
  these rites: a bald person cannot wear the mourning cap, a hunchback cannot bare
  their shoulder, a lame person cannot beat the ground — not because they lack sorrow,
  but because their physical condition prevents them from completing the ritual forms.
  Hence, the passage concludes: "Mourning rites take sorrow as their primary element."


  This concept reveals how *li* accommodates human diversity: while the ideal forms
  of mourning are prescribed, those genuinely unable to perform them are not condemned
  but are understood to have fulfilled the essential spirit of mourning through their
  sorrow. The external forms serve the internal emotion; when the emotion is sincere,
  even imperfect performance remains acceptable.'
