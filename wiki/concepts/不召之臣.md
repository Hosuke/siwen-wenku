孟子言古聖王之待臣下，以湯之於伊尹、桓公之於管仲，皆學焉而後臣之，不敢召也。蓋天下有達尊三：爵也，齒也，德也。朝廷之上莫如爵，鄉里之中莫如齒，而輔世長民者莫如德。君有其一，不可以慢其二。故大有為之君，必有所不召之臣；欲有謀焉，則就之。此尊德樂道之義，非虛己下問不足以有為也。

**所出**：《孟子 · 公孫丑下》 — https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/孟子/公孫丑下

## English

The concept "不召之臣" (the unsummonable minister) emerges from Mencius's discussion with Wan Zhang about why a scholar-official refuses audience with feudal lords. This principle holds that truly exemplary persons cannot be summoned at will; they must be approached according to proper ritual.

Mencius begins with the definition: within the city, one is a "market-place minister" (市井之臣); in the countryside, a "grassland minister" (草莽之臣)—both are commoners (庶人). A commoner who has not presented the ritual gift (傳質) as a subject dare not visit a lord—this is proper ritual (禮也). Yet if summoned for labor, one goes; but summoned for an audience, one does not go. Why? Because "going to serve is righteous; going to be seen is not" (往役，義也；往見，不義也).

When Wan Zhang asks whether a ruler might summon a worthy person for the sake of the ruler's own learning, Mencius replies sharply: "If for the sake of hearing much, then even the Son of Heaven does not summon a teacher—how much less a feudal lord! If for the sake of worthiness, I have never heard of wishing to see a worthy person and summoning him." The example of Zisi (子思) is telling: when Duke Mu of Wei urgently sought to befriend him, asking how a lord of a thousand chariots might befriend a scholar, Zisi was displeased. He responded that according to ancient practice, one serves (事之云乎), not befriends (友之云乎)—for by position, "you are lord and I am minister, how dare I befriend you? By virtue, you should serve me. How could you be my friend?" A lord of a thousand chariots sought friendship and could not obtain it; how much less could he summon?

The story of the forester (虞人) illustrates the point: Duke Jing of Qi announced a hunt, summoning the forester with a banner (旌). The forester would not come, and would have been killed.
