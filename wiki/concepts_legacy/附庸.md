## English

The **Vassal State** (附庸) represented the lowest tier in the ancient Chinese feudal hierarchy, comprising territories too small to maintain direct relations with the Son of Heaven. According to the *Book of Rites: Royal Regulations*, the land allocation system established five categories of territorial grants: the Son of Heaven received one thousand li square, dukes and marquises one hundred li square, earls seventy li square, and viscounts and barons fifty li square. Any territory unable to meet the fifty-li threshold was deemed unfit for direct connection with the Son of Heaven and was attached to a neighboring lord as a vassal state.

The text specifies that the various ranks were organized as follows: among feudal lords, the upper category included ministers (卿) and lower category included lower ministers (下大夫); among officials, there were upper scholars (上士), middle scholars (中士), and lower scholars (下士)—five grades in each case. The vassal states were categorized as *jiāng tián* (間田), or unassigned lands, and served to compensate lords who had been punished with territorial reduction.

The administrative geography organized these territories into nested hierarchies: five states constituted a *zhǔ* (屬), with a chief; ten states formed a *lián* (連), with a commander; thirty states made a *zú* (卒), with a supervisor; and two hundred ten states comprised a *zhōu* (州), with a hegemon. Eight such administrative regions were established, each supervised by a hegemon who reported to one of two supreme commanders (二伯) appointed by the Son of Heaven.

Within the royal domain itself, the arrangement differed: within the nine hundred li square surrounding the capital, there were ninety-three states—nine of one hundred li, twenty-one of seventy li, and sixty-three of fifty li. The total across the empire amounted to one thousand seven hundred seventy-three states, including the various grades.

The system demonstrates the intricate hierarchical thinking of ancient Chinese political philosophy, where even the smallest territorial units were assigned specific ranks and relationships within a cosmic order linking heaven and earth.

## 中文

**附庸**者，小國之附於大國者也。《禮記·王制》曰：「天子之田方千里，公侯田方百里，伯七十里，子男五十里。不能五十里者，不合於天子，附於諸侯曰附庸。」

是故附庸之制，所以處夫地狹人寡之國，使其有所統屬，不至於孤立也。附庸之祿視元士，不得與大國比肩，然亦得保有其宗廟社稷焉。

九州之制，大州百里之國三十，七十里之國六十，五十里之國百二十，凡二百一十國。名山大澤不以封，其餘以為附庸間田。八州，州二百一十國。

附庸又有間田之別。間田者，未分之田，待有功而封者也。《王制》又云：「諸侯之有功者，取於間田以祿之；其有削地者，歸之閒田。」是故削地之諸侯不失其祿，附庸之君亦有進升之機。

又有方伯之設。八州八伯，五十六正，百六十八帥，三百三十六長。八伯各以其屬屬於天子之老二人，分天下以為左右，曰二伯。千里之內曰甸，千里之外曰采，曰流。

由是觀之，封建之制，細密周詳，大小相維，內外相制，附庸之設亦其中不可闕者焉。

## 日本語

**附庸**とは、古代中国の封建制度における最下位の領土単位である。『礼記·王制』によれば天子は千里四方、公侯は百里四方、伯は七十里四方、子男は五十里四方の禄田を受けた。五十里未満の領土は天子に直接仕えることができず、諸侯に付屬された。これが附庸である。

附庸の禄は元士に準じ、直接天子に仕える大国の諸侯とは身分が異なった。しかしそれでも祖宗の祭祀を維持し、一定の自治権を保持した。

九州の行政構造においては、百里之国三十、七十里之国六十、五十里之国百二十の計二百十国が配置された。名山大沢は封じず、残りを附庸と閒田に当てた。功績ある者には閒田から禄を与え、領土を削られた者には閒田を当てて生計を維持させた。

八州には八伯が置き、五十六正、百六十八帥、三百三十六長を配置し、天子の老（大臣）二人（二伯）に統属させた。こうすることで、帝国の隅々まで秩序を維持する仕組みが構築された。
